Multiple heirs, one decision: keeping everyone on the same page

Siblings disagree about the house more than about anything else in an estate. How executors keep it from turning into a fight.

By Vennessa Mele, DRE #02115354 · Orange County, California · Last reviewed September 2026

Short answer: as personal representative you have the authority to sell, but you have a duty to all the heirs, and a sale that one heir resents can turn into an objection, a delay, or a lawsuit. Communicate early, put things in writing, and let the numbers do the arguing.

The usual disagreements

  • One sibling wants to keep the house; the others want cash.
  • One sibling is living in the house and is not paying rent.
  • One sibling thinks the house is worth far more than it is.
  • One sibling is out of state and feels left out of decisions.

What works

Share the same facts with everyone
Send every heir the appraisal, the comparable sales, and the monthly carrying cost of the house. Disagreements shrink when everyone is looking at the same numbers.
Use the Notice of Proposed Action
With full authority, you are required to give heirs 15 days' notice before the sale closes. Treat it as an opportunity, not a formality: send it with the offer summary and a note explaining why you accepted it.
Put buyouts in writing
If one heir wants to keep the house, they can buy the others out at appraised value, usually with a new loan. Get the terms and the timeline on paper. Open-ended "I'll figure out financing" arrangements are where estates stall.
Charge rent, or account for it
An heir living in the house owes the estate fair rent, or that value gets accounted for at distribution. Saying so early avoids a fight later.
Bring in a neutral voice
A single group call with the agent and, if needed, the attorney lets everyone ask their questions at once. I do these regularly and they defuse more than they inflame.

When you cannot agree

The court can resolve it. A petition for instructions asks the judge to approve a sale over an objection; a partition action forces a sale of co-owned property after distribution. Both are slow and expensive. Almost every estate does better settling it around a table first.
